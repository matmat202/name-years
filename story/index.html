Chapter 6 · Is it just spelling?

Aiden, Aidan, Ayden

Some of this is spelling. The record counts Jackson, Jaxon and Jaxson as three names. So count again with every set of names that sound alike folded into one, by the old Soundex code — which folds too much, merging Mary with Marie and Mark with Marco.

Chapter 7 · What did not change

Fads are not faster

It feels as if names now flare and die quicker. They do not. Take the names that were in their year's top twenty at their peak, for peaks from 1930 to 2009. In every decade from the 1940s to the 2000s, the typical hit took between and years to climb from a quarter of its peak to the top, and fifteen years later still held to of its peak share.

A name rises and falls at about the speed it always did. What changed is how many names are rising at once.

A first count said the opposite — that hits had grown far shorter-lived. That count kept only names that had already faded, which recent names have not had time to do.

Coda · Your name

Where is yours?

Type any first name into Name Years to see how many babies got it every year since 1880, when it peaked, and how old the people who have it are now.

Counts are the Social Security Administration's national baby-name file, which lists a name only if at least five babies of a sex got it in a year; shares here are of the babies it lists, so the rarest names are left out of both sides, and leaving them out understates how spread out recent names are. The story starts at 1940 because records for earlier births come from people who applied for a card later in life. Ranks are by exact spelling; the Soundex count is in chapter 6. Hits are names in their own peak year's top twenty, per sex, with peaks from 1930 to 2009 so that each has fifteen years of record after its peak.
